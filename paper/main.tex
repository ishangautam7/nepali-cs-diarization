\begin{abstract}
People who code-switch change language mid-utterance without changing who they are. We ask
whether a Nepali--English switch within one speaker lowers speaker-embedding similarity and
raises a speaker-change score. In a pre-specified analysis of real lecture speech from 17
YouTube channels (a proxy for speakers), we compare same-speaker windows that straddle a
switch with matched same-language controls, for three speaker embeddings. Between language
runs of at least 1~s, similarity is lower across a switch for all three embeddings and in
every channel ($d_z$ $0.19$--$0.27$), and cross-language same-speaker scores are
$0.17$--$0.22$ SD lower. A two-window change score is slightly higher at these switches (AUC
$0.57$--$0.58$, in a primary cell chosen after a pilot), but over all switches, most of which border shorter runs, there is no positive ranking separation (pooled AUC $\approx 0.49$). For two embeddings the excess survives the planned adjustment for measured acoustic covariates, although the observational
design cannot show that language causes it. On Hindi-- and Bengali--English (MUCS~2021) the
direction is the same. Two mitigations meant to remove language information reduced the excess by $18.9\%$ and $24.9\%$, inconclusively, and one made language more, not less, decodable. In a post hoc check, two off-the-shelf diarization
pipelines almost never reported a false speaker change at natural switches. The similarity drop is consistent but small, the evidence is preliminary (no independent listener has checked the switch points), and whether it matters in conversational diarization remains open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Speaker diarization relies on speaker embeddings and on scores that compare adjacent
windows. If a language switch by one speaker (SS-DL: \emph{same speaker, different
language}) moves the embedding, a diarization system could report a speaker change that is
not there. We test the first link of that chain. Is same-speaker embedding similarity lower,
and a two-window change score higher, at natural Nepali$\leftrightarrow$English switches
than at matched same-language boundaries of the same speaker? And do mitigations meant to remove language information from the embedding reduce that excess?

Cross-lingual speaker verification already shows that embeddings rate the same speaker as
less similar across languages~\cite{thienpondt2022,li2026}, and LASE~\cite{menta2026lase}
reports the same for synthetic Indic voices. Diarization studies of code-mixed speech report
corpus-level error~\cite{baghel2023displace}. What is missing is a measurement \emph{at
individual, natural switch points} in real speech, against matched same-speaker controls.
We provide one for Nepali--English. LASE also found that the cross-script gap is smaller for
Indian-accented voices, so for speakers whose first language is Nepali the effect may be
small; we fixed the analysis plan before the confirmatory run so that a small or null result could still be reported.

In short, between language runs of at least a second, same-speaker similarity is slightly
but consistently lower across a switch, and the change score slightly higher. Over all switches, most of which border a run shorter than half a second, there is no positive ranking separation, and neither testable mitigation demonstrably removed the excess we
found. The planned tests on full diarization pipelines and on real cross-lingual speaker
turns (DS-DL: \emph{different speaker, different language}) needed conversation data we
could not obtain, so this is a diagnostic study of embeddings and change scores, with a
post hoc pipeline check.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A boundary-level protocol that compares speaker-embedding similarity and a
        two-window change score at same-speaker language switches with matched
        same-language controls, adjusted for measured acoustic covariates.
  \item Pre-specified results on real Nepali--English speech for three speaker
        embeddings (\S\ref{sec:results}), with a check of direction on Hindi-- and

  \item A pre-specified test of two mitigations meant to remove language information (\S\ref{sec:mitigations})

\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Code-switching.}
Linguistic work distinguishes the insertion of single words or constituents into a matrix
language from alternation between longer stretches of two
languages~\cite{poplack1980,muysken2000}. Do\u{g}ru\"{o}z et al.~\cite{dogruoz2021survey}
survey code-switching for language technologies, and the code-mixing index (CMI) measures
how mixed a text is~\cite{gamback2016cmi}. Most of our switch points are insertion-like
(\S\ref{sec:discussion}).

\textbf{Cross-lingual speaker representations.}
Speaker embeddings underestimate same-speaker similarity across languages, and probing shows
that they also encode spoken content and channel~\cite{raj2019probing}. Thienpondt et
al.~\cite{thienpondt2022} correct this score shift with language information. Later work
removes language with subspace normalisation~\cite{hosseini2026} or gradient-reversal
adversarial training~\cite{menta2026lase,li2026}. Hosseini-Kivanani~\cite{hosseini2026} and
Li et al.~\cite{li2026} evaluate verification only; Li et al.\ train on real multilingual
speech and found that added TTS speech did not improve verification. LASE~\cite{menta2026lase}
trains on TTS-generated cross-script pairs and adds a synthetic diarization benchmark of 50
conversations built from 2--4 TTS voices, one of which may switch language, scored by
clustering agreement with segments and speaker count given. It also reports that the
cross-script gap of off-the-shelf encoders is smaller for synthetic Indian-accented voices
(0.006 vs.\ 0.082 for WavLM-SV; 0.044 vs.\ 0.105 for ECAPA-TDNN).

\textbf{Diarization.}
Modern systems combine a powerset segmentation model~\cite{plaquet2023} with embedding
clustering: DiariZen pairs WavLM-based segmentation with WeSpeaker embeddings and VBx-style
clustering~\cite{han2025diarizen,landini2022vbx}, and DiaPer uses perceiver
attractors~\cite{landini2024diaper}. The DISPLACE
challenges~\cite{baghel2023displace,displace2024} report diarization error on multilingual
Indian conversations, DISPLACE-M extends this to code-switched medical
speech~\cite{displacem2026,barahona2026}, and the closest Nepali work evaluates multilingual
diarizers on Nepali--Hindi speech~\cite{neupane2026}. These studies report corpus-level
error; none of them, nor LASE's synthetic benchmark, measures embedding similarity or a
change score at individual natural switch points. Mishra and Prasanna~\cite{mishra2023}
detect spoken-language change by analogy with speaker-change detection, which suggests that
the two are acoustically entangled; our acoustic covariates and pause controls address part
of this.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}
\subsection{D1: Nepali--English lectures (main corpus)}
We use a Nepali--English code-switched corpus of STEM lectures~\cite{dahal2026csasr}:
20{,}805 clips (58.4~h, 3--14~s each) from 161 videos across 20 YouTube channels, at
16~kHz. Its transcripts are mostly machine-generated (777 clips are human-verified), so
language tags and switch points inherit transcription errors. The dataset's speaker label is
the \emph{channel}, not a verified person, so we audit speaker purity (\S\ref{sec:purity}).
We use only the real (non-augmented) clips. The 3 validation channels are the pilot, used to
fix every threshold; the confirmatory analysis runs once on the other 17 channels (15
training, 2 test).

\subsection{D4: MUCS 2021 Hindi/Bengali--English (direction check)}
To check the direction of the effect in other language pairs, we use the MUCS~2021
code-switched subsets~\cite{diwan2021mucs}: Hindi--English (52{,}825 train and 3{,}136 test
clips) and Bengali--English (26{,}606 and 4{,}275), cut from narrated computer-science
tutorials. The MUCS authors had no speaker information and assume that each tutorial has a
different speaker~\cite{diwan2021mucs}, so a MUCS ``speaker'' is a tutorial and one narrator
may appear under many IDs. MUCS often writes English words in native script (e.g.\
``office'' in Devanagari), so we tag language with a per-script lexicon of 763 loans and 67
names for Hindi and 393 and 21 for Bengali, labelled by an LLM and spot-checked as text.
This gives 4{,}267 (Hindi) and 2{,}769 (Bengali) switch points with matched controls for H1,
and 5{,}071 and 3{,}510 with cell controls in the primary detection cell.

\subsection{Data we could not obtain}
\label{sec:data-conv}
The planned tests of full diarization pipelines need long-form recordings, and the
DS-DL tests need conversations with references for both speaker and language. D1's source
videos could not be downloaded, access to DISPLACE~2024~\cite{displace2024} was not granted
during the study, and DISPLACE-M~\cite{displacem2026} has no language references. These
tests were therefore not run: the full-pipeline variants of the change-score test (H2), whether the mitigations add missed cross-language speaker turns (H5), and their ordering (H6).  Apart from the
artificial speaker changes of \S\ref{sec:pipe}, no result here involves a speaker change.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Switch points, controls and matching}
We romanise each word with uroman~\cite{hermjakob2018uroman}, force-align each clip with
torchaudio's MMS\_FA bundle (MMS-300M~\cite{pratap2023mms}), using
Whisper~\cite{radford2023whisper} word timestamps as a cross-check, tag each word's language
from its script with a loan/name lexicon, and detect pauses with Silero
VAD~\cite{silero2021vad}. An SS-DL switch point is the boundary between two adjacent words of
the same speaker in different languages, with both words aligned and no neutral or loan word
nearby. Each point is assigned to a run-length stratum ($<0.5$, $0.5$--$1$, $\geq 1$~s) by
the shorter adjacent language run.

Two analysis families use the points; a third test (H3, \S\ref{sec:stats}) compares cross-language with mono-language same-speaker trials. The \emph{embedding} family (H1) compares each switch
point between runs of at least 1~s with up to three same-language control boundaries from
the same clip or video, matched exactly on pause bin, position bin and context length; its
1~s windows exclude any pause. The \emph{detection} family (H2-P0, for the change score P0 below) compares switch points
with all same-speaker controls in the same video $\times$ pause-bin $\times$ position-bin
cell, standardised per cell; its 1~s windows meet at the middle of any pause.

\emph{Primary detection cell.} The primary cell, \texttt{p0L1}, keeps switch points where
both language runs last at least 1~s, with a clean $\pm1$~s field: on the confirmatory
channels, 3{,}962 switch points with cell controls, against 31{,}844 controls. The pooled
cell holds all 120{,}630 switch points. The initial study design, committed before the pilot, made the pooled cell primary. On the pilot channels its AUC was $0.486$--$0.494$, while switch points between longer runs fired more often than controls. We then made \texttt{p0L1} primary, because a detector with 1~s windows cannot register a sub-second insertion as a two-sided change; this also gives P0 the run-length condition the initial design already fixed for H1. This change was made after seeing pilot outcomes. The formal analysis plan was written after it, names \texttt{p0L1} as primary, and was frozen with the configuration before the one confirmatory run; the confirmatory channels are disjoint from the pilot. Because the primary cell shows a positive result where the pooled cell shows no positive separation, we report both.

\subsection{Systems}
We test three speaker embeddings: E1 ECAPA-TDNN~\cite{desplanques2020ecapa}, E2 the
WeSpeaker ResNet34-LM embedding~\cite{wang2023wespeaker} of pyannote Community-1, and E3
WavLM-Large + ECAPA~\cite{chen2022wavlm}. The change score P0 is $d(t)=1-\cos$ between the
1~s windows before and after each position on a dense grid. Within $\pm0.5$~s of a boundary
it keeps the highest peak and \emph{fires} when that peak exceeds an operating point $\tau$,
set on the pilot so that 10\% of controls fire. $\Delta$FAR is how much more often P0 fires
at switch points than at their cell controls. Every fire at a same-speaker boundary is a
false alarm, so $\Delta$FAR is a change-score false-alarm excess, not a diarization error
rate. In the primary cell P0 compares windows much like H1's, so its AUC largely restates
H1's similarity drop as a ranking; what P0 adds is peak picking and an operating point.

To check that switch points carry a language change, we scored each boundary on the pilot
channels by the Jensen--Shannon divergence between language-ID
posteriors~\cite{valk2021voxlingua} of the 1~s windows on either side. It ranked switch
points above controls with AUC 0.58 overall and 0.72 where the shorter run lasts at least
1~s, but it also ranks pause-bearing controls above other controls (AUC 0.56), so it is only
a weak check.

\subsection{Speaker-purity audit}
\label{sec:purity}
Because a speaker is a channel, we audit each video with two embeddings not under test
(TitaNet-L and CAM++), comparing each clip only with same-video clips of similar language
mix (by CMI tertile~\cite{gamback2016cmi}), so that English-heavy clips are not flagged for
their language. The audit flagged 688 of the 20{,}444 clips assessable by both embeddings (3.4\%), at the same rate in every
language-mix stratum, and split off 34 of 161 videos, most of them separate recording
sessions of one teacher. Apart from one pilot-channel video that may share a teacher with a
confirmatory channel, the closest pair of videos from two channels has a cosine of 0.65 (TitaNet-L) and 0.66 (CAM++),
below the 0.77--0.95 typical of two videos of one channel. In a blind listening check of 117
pairs by the author, the main voice of a flagged clip was judged to differ from its video's
usual speaker in 51\% of usable pairs (95\% CI 41--62\%), against 8\% for unflagged control pairs (1 of 12; 95\% CI 1--35\%). With flagged clips dropped and split-off videos as separate clusters, every primary
result keeps its direction and meets its smallest effect of interest (H1 $d_z$
0.25/0.21/0.26, H2-P0 AUC 0.58/0.56/0.59, H3 $-0.24/-0.20/-0.25$~SD for E1/E2/E3).

\subsection{Mitigations}
\label{sec:mit-method}
The plan specified three mitigations and three baselines. M1 projects the language direction
out of the embedding~\cite{hosseini2026}. M2 gates the change score with language
ID~\cite{valk2021voxlingua}. M3 fine-tunes E3 on real speech only, with a gradient-reversal
language loss~\cite{ganin2015grl}, AAM-softmax~\cite{deng2019arcface}, a
supervised-contrastive loss and distillation towards E3, adding a Nepali speaker pool from
IndicVoices~\cite{javed2024indicvoices}, with five seeds per fold. The baselines are B0, the
unmodified system with its operating point recalibrated; B1, language-wise mean centring;
and B2, a language-aware score offset~\cite{thienpondt2022}. The test (H4) uses P0 with E3
on the 15 training channels in 5 folds of 3: every component is fitted on the other 12
channels and every $\tau$ is set on the pilot. For each channel, $\delta_M =
\Delta\mathrm{FAR}_M - \Delta\mathrm{FAR}_{B0}$ is tested by an exact one-sided sign-flip
with Holm correction over M1--M3; support also needs a reduction of at least 25\% of B0's
excess. M2 could not be tested: its gate weight was to be tuned on conversations, because on
D1, which has no speaker changes, any gate lowers false alarms at no visible cost. For the
same reason, a lower excess alone is not an improvement.

\subsection{Statistics}
\label{sec:stats}
The unit of analysis is the channel. We use exact sign-flip permutation tests over the 17
confirmatory channels ($2^{17}$ sign patterns), BCa bootstrap intervals at $1-0.05/m$, and
Holm correction within each hypothesis family ($m=3$ for H1 and H3; $m=5$ for H2, whose
untested pipeline variants count at $p=1$). For H1, let $x_{ci}$ be the cosine drop (control minus switch) of matched set $i$ in channel $c$, with $n_c$ sets, mean $\bar{x}_c$ and SD $s_c$, over $C=17$ channels. We define
\begin{equation}
d_z=\frac{\frac{1}{C}\sum_c \bar{x}_c}{s_p},\qquad
s_p=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_c (n_c-1)\,s_c^2}{\sum_c (n_c-1)}},
\end{equation}
an equal-weight mean of channel means over the pooled within-channel SD of the set-level drops. This differs from the conventional $d_z$ (mean paired difference over the SD of those differences at the analysis unit): we use set-level variability because that is the noise a detector faces at each boundary, and the SD of 17 channel means would be unstable. The smallest effect of interest (0.2) and the power calculation use this same definition, and the BCa interval recomputes $s_p$ in every resample. For H2-P0, a channel's AUC is the probability that a switch point's $d(t)$ exceeds a
same-cell control's. H3 is the mean target-score difference (cross-language minus
mono-language) on lag-matched same-speaker trials, in units of target-score SD, with the two
languages weighted equally. The smallest effects of interest are $d_z=0.2$, AUC $=0.55$ and
0.2~SD; a result is \emph{supported} if it is significant with a point estimate at least this
large. The sample was fixed by the data; with pilot variance components, the planned power at these effects was 0.68--1.00 for H1 and 0.70--0.76 for H2-P0.

An excess is \emph{covariate-robust} if the primary test, a channel-level test adjusted for
acoustic covariates (left--right changes in F0, energy, speaking rate, spectral tilt and
SNR), a contrast against same-language phrase boundaries (pause $>150$~ms) and H3 are all
significant; otherwise it is \emph{switch-associated}. Reverberation, prosodic phrasing and
syntactic position were not measured, so this grade shows an association that survives
adjustment, not that language causes it.

\textbf{The excess depends on run length.} Over all switch points there is no positive ranking separation (pooled AUC 0.485--0.493), and at $\tau$ the pooled $\Delta$FAR is negative ($-1.59/-1.67/-1.51$~pp for E1/E2/E3; secondary, no test). The pooled AUC rises from $\approx 0.48$ when the shorter
run is under 0.5~s to $\approx 0.59$ when it is at least 1~s (exploratory), consistent with a
1~s detector being unable to register short insertions. Window length behaves similarly for
H1: with 0.5~s windows $d_z$ falls to 0.13/0.10/0.12 (E1/E2/E3), and with 1.5~s windows it
rises to 0.34/0.24/0.36, though longer windows also admit only longer runs.

\textbf{Size in practical terms.} On H3's trials the shift costs 0.6--0.9~pp of equal error rate (EER)
(12.9/9.5/11.4\% cross-language against 12.2/8.9/10.5\% mono-language). At $\tau$, P0 fires at
13--17\% of primary-cell switch points but at 22--25\% of same-language boundaries with a
pause over 150~ms (channel means, not cell-standardised), so for this detector a pause is a larger source of within-speaker false alarms than a language switch. The operating-point excess is also uneven across channels (Fig.~\ref{fig:det}):
$\Delta$FAR is positive in 9, 12 and 13 of 17 channels, and weighting switch points rather
than channels equally gives $+0.78$, $+2.00$ and $+1.17$~pp, because the largest channel has a negative $\Delta$FAR and the largest values come from small channels.

\textbf{Acoustic controls.} H1 is covariate-robust for all three embeddings. The H2-P0
excess is covariate-robust for E2 ($p=.031$) and E3 ($p=.008$) and switch-associated for E1
($p=.079$). A descriptive mixed model agrees for H1 (switch effect $-0.030/-0.024/-0.031$ in
cosine, $|z|\geq 7.3$, almost unchanged without covariates) but finds the H2-P0 excess weak
(one-sided $p$ .081/.026/.124).

\textbf{Robustness.} Every outcome category is unchanged when low-confidence alignments are
dropped or the 2 test channels are left out, and no H2-P0 or H3 category changes when switch
points are re-extracted under three alternative rules (two tagging changes, and keeping low-alignment clips); only E2's H1, which sits at its smallest effect throughout ($d_z$ 0.17--0.21), moves between grades, when the low-alignment clips are kept. Leaving out any one
channel (post hoc) reverses no estimate and leaves every primary test significant; the least
stable quantity is E1's $\Delta$FAR, which falls to $+1.17$~pp ($p=.09$) without its
smallest channel (13 switch points).

\textbf{Listening check.} The author listened blind to 352 items. No language change was
audible at 6.3\% of primary-cell switch points (95\% CI 2.0--11.6\%), none had a different
speaker or overlap, and no control had a language change. Correcting $\Delta$FAR for this
contamination raises it by about 7\%. Fig.~\ref{fig:umap} illustrates the embedding geometry behind H1 and H3.

\section{Direction Check on Hindi-- and Bengali--English}
\label{sec:h7}
We repeat H1 and H2-P0 on D4, specified and frozen before any D4 outcome was computed,
with MUCS speaker IDs (tutorials) as clusters. A tutorial enters a test if it has at least 5 matched sets (H1) or 5 primary-cell switch points (H2-P0): 364 and 394 of 550 Hindi IDs, and 235 and 254 of 307 Bengali IDs. With hundreds of clusters, the sign-flip draws $2^{17}$
random sign patterns. All 12 tests are significant in the predicted direction (Holm
one-sided $p \le 2.6\times10^{-3}$; Table~\ref{tab:h7}). In Hindi--English the estimates are
comparable to D1; in Bengali--English every point estimate falls below D1's smallest effect,
so there the direction matches, but the effect is not shown to reach D1's practical size.

clusters. With so few clusters no Holm-adjusted $p$ can fall below $.05$ (the smallest attainable is $\approx 0.19$), so no test can reach significance; this reflects a lack of power, not evidence against the effect, and the check is uninformative. Robustness
to speaker dependence is therefore untested on D4, which supports the direction of the
effect, not its significance under realistic speaker dependence. An LLM-labelled text audit of 200 native-tagged tokens per pair put residual English at 0.5\% for Hindi and 1.0\% for Bengali; re-reading the Bengali items found four missed English words, which raises it to 3.0\%, probably the fairer figure.

\section{Exploratory Full-Pipeline Check}
\label{sec:pipe}
After all planned analyses, we ran two off-the-shelf diarization pipelines with
default settings and no given speaker count: pyannote Community-1, with its VBx
clustering~\cite{landini2022vbx}, and DiariZen~\cite{han2025diarizen}. On the 17
confirmatory channels we drew 300 primary-cell switch points and one same-cell control for
each, and ran each pipeline on the whole clip. Because D1 has no speaker changes, we also
built artificial ones: 300 joins of clips from two channels with the same language at the
join, and 300 with a language change; 300 joins of consecutive clips from one video serve as
same-speaker joins. A join also carries a recording cut, so it tests whether a speaker change
is found, not natural turn-taking. A boundary counts as a reported change if the output
switches speaker within $\pm0.5$~s.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
On real Nepali--English speech, same-speaker embedding similarity is lower across a
language switch (H1), and cross-language same-speaker scores are lower (H3), confirming at
natural switch points the shift known from verification~\cite{thienpondt2022,menta2026lase}.
The shift survives the measured acoustic and pause controls, and it is small: standardised
effects of 0.17--0.27, or a cosine drop of 0.037/0.029/0.034 (E1/E2/E3). For ECAPA-TDNN this
is close to LASE's off-the-shelf gap for synthetic Indian-accented voices (0.044) and well
below that for Western-accented voices (0.105), although LASE's gaps are utterance-level and
ours come from 1~s windows.

The change score tells a narrower story. It is higher at switches only where both language
runs last at least a second. Most switches in these lectures are
insertion-like~\cite{muysken2000}: 79\% of the 120{,}630 pooled switch points border a run
shorter than 0.5~s, too short for a 1~s detector to resolve, and over all switches there is no positive ranking separation. The two off-the-shelf pipelines rarely reported a false change at
switches. Neither mitigation demonstrably removed the excess, and neither demonstrated an advantage over the simple score offset (pre-specified secondary comparisons with the offset, exact one-sided sign-flip over the 15 channels, unadjusted $p=.67$ and $.54$). In the short lecture clips and pipeline configurations tested here, language switches by one speaker therefore look like a minor source of false speaker changes; the artificial joins do not establish how these pipelines respond to natural conversational speaker changes. Whether that holds in conversation, and whether
a mitigation would cost missed real speaker changes, needs conversations with speaker and
language references.

\textbf{Limitations.} No planned pipeline or DS-DL test could be run, so the results
mainly concern embeddings and change scores. The primary detection cell was chosen after
the pilot. The design is observational. D1's speakers are YouTube channels and D4's are
tutorials, neither verified, and the channel-level tests assume they are independent. The
listening checks had one rater, the author, who knew the hypotheses; a subset for two
independent bilingual raters is prepared but not yet labelled, so the evidence is
preliminary. D1's transcripts are mostly machine-generated, and the planned measurement of tag noise on its 777 human-verified clips was not run; the D4 language labels were checked only as text. Both corpora are instructional monologues without speaker metadata
such as gender, and switching in conversation may differ in frequency, run length and
prosody. With 15 channels, H4 could not separate a 25\% reduction from none.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Within one speaker and between language runs of at least a second, three speaker embeddings
rate the same voice as slightly less similar across a natural Nepali--English switch, and a
two-window change score is slightly higher there. Over all switches there is no positive ranking separation, two mitigations reduced the excess only inconclusively, and two off-the-shelf pipelines rarely reported a false speaker change at
switches. Testing the effect in conversational diarization needs long-form conversations
with speaker and language references.

\section{Ethics, Data Availability and Use of AI}
\label{sec:ethics}
The study uses only publicly released recordings: D1 (CC BY-NC 4.0)~\cite{dahal2026csasr},
MUCS~2021 (CC BY-SA 4.0) and the Nepali part of IndicVoices (CC BY 4.0). It makes no new
recordings, recruits no participants and redistributes no audio, so no ethics-board review
was sought. Speaker embeddings are biometric data, so only aggregate statistics are
reported. Code, the analysis plan and all results are available at the repository above. The plan was written and committed to the project's git repository before the confirmatory analyses, and each confirmatory analysis was frozen as a tagged commit, recording a hash of every code and input file, before it was run. We call the plan pre-specified rather than pre-registered because it was not deposited with an external registry; the full commit history is available on request. Post hoc analyses are labelled as such.

The author designed the study, wrote the analysis plan, made the analysis decisions, did
the listening checks and interpreted the results. AI assistants (Anthropic Claude models)
helped write and run the analysis code and edit the text. LLM-based tools labelled the D4 lexicon and its audit sample, as text only. The author checked their output and is responsible for all content.
